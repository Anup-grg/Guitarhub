# Website UI/UX Design Requirements

**A project brief for designing a useful, distinctive, and production-ready website**
**Reference window:** Awarded and featured work published in 2026

## What this brief is for

Use this document to plan, design, build, and review a website. It is intentionally broad enough to suit a product, service, publication, campaign, portfolio, or store. Complete the project-specific decisions before visual design begins; do not treat every item as a reason to add another page or feature.

The standard is straightforward: a visitor should quickly understand what the site offers, find the next useful action, and be able to complete it comfortably on the device and connection they have.

## 1. Project definition

Record the answers in the project brief and keep them available to design, content, and engineering.

- **Purpose:** What should the website help the organization or audience do?
- **Primary audience:** Who is it for? Include relevant needs, context, accessibility considerations, and familiarity with the subject.
- **Top visitor tasks:** List the three to five things people are most likely to come to do, in their own words.
- **Primary action:** State the most valuable next step for a visitor. Examples: compare plans, book a consultation, buy an item, or read a report.
- **Success measures:** Choose observable measures tied to those tasks, such as successful form submissions, completed purchases, qualified enquiries, or task completion in research.
- **Scope:** Name the pages, languages, integrations, and content types needed for launch. Mark later work separately.
- **Constraints:** Note launch date, budget, legal requirements, content dependencies, existing brand rules, and technical limits.

Do not begin with a style direction such as "make it modern." Begin with the audience, the offer, and the work the website must do.

## 2. Research and content

- Review the current site, customer questions, analytics, support requests, and search terms where available.
- Speak with representative users or stakeholders. Validate assumptions about vocabulary, trust, and the decisions visitors need to make.
- Map each top task to the pages and content that support it. Remove pages that have no audience need or clear owner.
- Give every page one primary purpose, a clear title, and a useful next step.
- Put important facts where a visitor needs them: pricing near the offer, eligibility near the application, delivery terms near checkout, and evidence near claims.
- Use plain language, concrete headings, short paragraphs, and descriptive links. Explain unfamiliar terms at the point of use.
- Support claims with current, attributable evidence. Do not invent testimonials, customer logos, statistics, awards, or guarantees.
- Agree who owns each content area, how often it should be reviewed, and what happens when it is outdated.

### Recommended content map

Adapt this to the project; it is not a mandatory sitemap.

| Page or area                    | Visitor question it should answer                              |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Home or landing page            | What is this, who is it for, and where should I start?         |
| Offer, product, or service      | What do I get, how does it work, and is it right for me?       |
| Pricing or purchase             | What will it cost, what is included, and what happens next?    |
| About, team, or credentials     | Who is behind this, and why should I trust them?               |
| Work, evidence, or case studies | Can you show relevant results or examples?                     |
| Help, FAQ, or policies          | What if I have a concern, special case, or need more detail?   |
| Contact or conversion flow      | How do I take the next step, and what information is required? |

## 3. Information architecture and navigation

- Organize pages around visitors' language and tasks, not internal department names.
- Keep primary navigation concise. Use labels that describe the destination; avoid vague labels such as "Discover" when a clearer one fits.
- Make the current location apparent on multi-page sites. Provide breadcrumbs when they improve orientation in a deep hierarchy.
- Keep the logo linked to the home page, provide a clear route to contact or support, and make the primary action easy to find.
- On mobile, use a menu control with an accessible name and visible open/closed state. Keep essential actions available without forcing repeated scrolling.
- Provide a useful 404 page with a way back to a relevant page or search.
- If the site has search, make its scope clear, support empty and no-result states, and let people recover from misspellings or broad queries.

## 4. Page and interaction design

- Establish a visual hierarchy through type, spacing, contrast, and grouping. A visitor should be able to scan the page before reading every word.
- Place a clear page heading and a concise explanation near the top. The first screen should orient people, not hide the purpose behind a slogan or animation.
- Use one visually clear primary action per decision area. Secondary actions should look secondary and say what they do.
- Design complete states for controls: default, hover, focus, pressed, disabled, loading, success, and error where relevant.
- Make feedback immediate and specific. Confirm what happened and tell the visitor what to do if an action fails.
- Keep forms short. Ask only for information needed at that step; label fields persistently, explain formats, and put errors beside the relevant field.
- Preserve entered form data when validation fails. Use appropriate input types, autocomplete, and mobile-friendly controls.
- For destructive or costly actions, explain the consequence before the final confirmation.
- Use dialogs, carousels, accordions, and sticky elements only when they improve the task. Ensure they remain usable by keyboard, touch, and assistive technology.
- Never rely on color, position, sound, or motion alone to communicate essential information.

## 5. Visual direction

Create a recognizable visual point of view that belongs to the organization and its audience.

- Define a small design system for color, typography, spacing, grids, borders, imagery, and component states. Document the rules so new pages remain consistent.
- Choose typefaces for legibility, language coverage, and available weights. Keep body text comfortable to read and line lengths manageable.
- Use color with enough contrast for text and controls. Reserve strong accent colors for actions and meaningful emphasis.
- Select photography, illustration, or product imagery that is specific to the subject. Use real, accurate assets when they carry more trust than generic stock imagery.
- Crop and art-direct images deliberately for each viewport. Provide useful alternative text for informative images and empty alternative text for decoration.
- Use iconography consistently. Pair unfamiliar icons with text labels.
- Make whitespace serve hierarchy and pace; do not use oversized empty areas to conceal a lack of content.
- Check that the visual system remains clear in long pages, dense information, and error states—not only in a polished hero section.

## 6. Responsive behavior

Design for content and input differences, not a set of device mockups alone.

- Support narrow and wide viewports, portrait and landscape, browser zoom, and text enlargement without loss of content or functionality.
- Reflow layouts naturally. Avoid horizontal scrolling for ordinary page content.
- Keep tap targets comfortably sized and separated. Do not make hover the only way to reveal a control.
- Verify menus, tables, charts, maps, media, dialogs, and forms on touch screens.
- Make images and video responsive, with appropriate crops and sources for different screen sizes.
- Test with real content, including long headings, translated strings, missing images, and unusually long names.
